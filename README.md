# Problems Worth Solving

> We are building a country of geniuses in a datacenter.  
> Someone still has to decide what it should care about.

Humanity has spent decades publishing lists of the most powerful, wealthy and influential people on Earth.

This repository asks a different question:

## What are the most important problems they—and increasingly capable AI systems—should be working on?

**Problems Worth Solving** is an open, evidence-backed registry of consequential problems that can be understood, commissioned and acted upon.

Not a list of fashionable causes.  
Not a graveyard of startup ideas.  
Not “solve climate change” written on a digital sticky note.

A problem belongs here when we can say:

- who is affected;
- what is going wrong;
- what a better outcome would look like;
- what has already been tried;
- what intelligence could unlock;
- what could go wrong;
- who has the authority or incentive to commission the work;
- and what a serious team could do next.

## Why now?

For most of history, intelligence was scarce.

A rare expert could think deeply about only a few problems, work limited hours and communicate through painfully narrow channels. Institutions emerged to allocate that scarce intelligence—but naturally directed most of it toward problems with money, power or prestige attached.

That constraint is beginning to change.

AI systems can already research, analyse, simulate, translate, design, code and coordinate at extraordinary speed. Soon, millions of capable agents may be working continuously and in parallel.

But abundant intelligence does not automatically produce abundant wisdom.

If nobody deliberately commissions work on maternal deaths, groundwater, learning poverty, neglected diseases or broken public systems, intelligence will continue flowing toward better advertisements, faster trading and more addictive feeds.

**The bottleneck may no longer be intelligence. It may be attention, specification and permission.**

This repository is an attempt to build that missing layer.

## The unit is a problem—not a solution

“Build an AI tutor” is a solution.

“Thirty children in this classroom read at ten different levels, while one teacher has no practical way to diagnose and respond to each child” is a problem.

Starting with the problem keeps us open to solutions that may involve AI, better institutions, policy, incentives, infrastructure—or no technology at all.

## How problems are evaluated

| Dimension | The question |
|---|---|
| Human value | How much suffering could be reduced or human potential unlocked? |
| Scale | How many people, communities, species or systems are affected? |
| Neglectedness | How large is the gap between importance and present attention? |
| Tractability | Can meaningful progress be made with current or near-term capabilities? |
| Measurability | Would we know if an intervention worked? |
| Leverage | Could progress unlock progress elsewhere? |
| Safety readiness | Are rights, risks, misuse and reversibility understood? |
| Commissionability | Is there a plausible actor with authority, resources and incentive to act? |

Scores are navigational aids—not moral truth. Each score must be accompanied by reasoning and evidence. A single universal ranking would conceal the very judgments this project should expose.

## How to participate

You can contribute as a:

- **Problem steward** — maintain one problem as the evidence evolves.
- **Domain reviewer** — test the evidence and feasibility.
- **Community reviewer** — represent people who live with the problem.
- **Builder** — propose or test a bounded intervention.
- **Commissioner** — fund, procure, host or enable the work.
- **Sceptic** — identify weak assumptions, duplication and unintended consequences.

Start with [CONTRIBUTING.md](CONTRIBUTING.md), then use the [problem template](templates/problem-template.md).

## The commissioning test

Every entry must confront the unfashionable but decisive question:

> If this problem is worth solving, who can give someone the permission, resources, data and accountability to solve it?

Possible commissioners include governments, foundations, companies, research institutions, multilaterals, communities and coalitions. Some problems will require new commissioning mechanisms. Making that gap visible is itself valuable.

## Principles

1. **People before prestige.** Attention is not impact.
2. **Problems before products.** Describe reality before prescribing technology.
3. **Evidence before virality.** Claims should survive scrutiny.
4. **Agency before automation.** Affected people are participants, not raw material.
5. **Specificity creates possibility.** A bounded problem can be acted upon.
6. **Open by default, protected when necessary.** Privacy and safety can require limits.
7. **Progress over performance.** The goal is not to appear intelligent. It is to make something meaningfully better.
8. **Hope is a discipline.** We take large problems seriously enough to define the next small move.

## What success looks like

The repository succeeds when problems move—not when the list grows.

A strong entry should eventually accumulate:

- validated evidence;
- affected-community insight;
- datasets and access routes;
- funders and commissioners;
- credible teams;
- experiments and results;
- failed approaches worth remembering;
- and a transparent record of progress.

Over time, this could become a searchable map connecting consequential problems with intelligence, capital, institutions and people who care enough to act.

## Origin

The project began with a simple response to the idea of “a country of geniuses in a datacenter”:

**Who is commissioning the ever-growing list of problems these geniuses are solving?**

This repository is our attempt to begin that list.
