# Have accessible opportunities to create, play and celebrate with others

> Illustrative wish, not a research quote: “I want somewhere I can make things, dance and enjoy being alive with other people.”

[All problems](../../CATALOGUE.md) · Feel connected and at peace · PWS-0225

## What a better life would feel like

A person can join or help shape shared music, dance, storytelling, play or an encounter with nature without prohibitive cost, inaccessible equipment or pressure to perform. They can celebrate the universe or explore spiritual meaning on their own terms, including through secular activities.

This differs from [having someone to turn to](PWS-0219.md): dependable relationships are valuable, but a creative experience can matter without producing a friendship or treating loneliness. Someone already well connected may still lack a place to create. Participants decide what the experience is for.

## The problem, in measurable parts

| What needs to change | What we would measure |
|---|---|
| Make participation practically possible | Total participant cost, travel and preparation time; access requests fulfilled; invitations, declined places, cancellations and attendance with reasons voluntarily supplied. |
| Keep creative agency with participants | Progress toward one self-chosen creative goal; whether participants could change the activity, decline suggestions and contribute in a preferred way. |
| Make the experience worth choosing | Optional reports of enjoyment and connection, alongside pressure, discomfort and desire to participate again. Report these separately. |

Do not combine these into a universal happiness score. Attendance, content volume, facial expression and time using a tool do not establish wellbeing. Missing feedback is unknown, not satisfaction.

## Where AI could help

Participants might direct tools to generate a musical accompaniment, adapt a story or visual scene, or reduce the host's coordination work. Save enough configuration and prompts to describe the intervention, without collecting private conversations. Log outages and human corrections.

The [holodeck as described by Star Trek](https://www.startrek.com/news/begin-program-the-reality-of-building-a-holodeck-today) inspires shared imaginary settings. This proposal tests ordinary creative tools, not the fictional machinery. Use speakers and an ordinary screen where helpful; neither VR nor an individual device is required. Compare with existing instruments, art materials and conventional media of equivalent cost.

## What technology cannot fix on its own

Venue access, transport, money, welcoming hosts and cultural trust may dominate the problem. A tool cannot confer consent to use someone's voice, story or spiritual tradition. Participants can refuse AI suggestions or choose an entirely conventional activity without losing their place.

## How the benefit must reach the person

Easier preparation should mean more accessible participation or greater participant control, rather than only a cheaper event for its operator. Report host workload as well as participant burden. Account for care and transport costs that might make a nominally free event inaccessible.

## A first experiment

**Proposed feasibility design, not an efficacy trial:** recruit 24 consenting adults through one community venue for six weekly 90-minute sessions, with a four-week follow-up. Record outreach routes and eligible people unable to attend; the number is an operating assumption, not a powered sample size.

Before allocation, participants choose a shared theme and an individual creative goal. Randomly allocate consenting participants within compatible time slots into two groups of 12. If accessibility or scheduling prevents random allocation, document the allocation process and treat comparisons as descriptive. Allow people to choose a non-study activity instead.

One group has optional participant-directed AI tools; the other uses conventional materials. Provide the same facilitator, equal preparation-time allowance, venue quality, fee, transport/access support and equipment budget. Hold separate sessions and alternate which group meets first each week. Preserve equivalent facilitator attention; record spillover and any additional technical assistance. No one is required to use generated material.

Collect baseline goals and access needs; attendance and full costs each week; optional brief experience reports after sessions; and goal/agency feedback at week six and four weeks later. Follow up consenting nonattenders and withdrawals once, with an easy refusal. Retain all allocated participants in participation denominators; report survey response denominators by group and time. Never fill missing enjoyment scores with attendance data.

Compare distributions and individual experiences, not just averages. With two small groups and one host, group dynamics and novelty can dominate differences. The next-stage decision is whether the AI option offers a participant-valued benefit beyond the conventional activity at an acceptable full cost. Agree what improvement would matter with participants before sessions; the current draft supplies no numerical efficacy threshold. Equal or worse results support using the simpler activity. This pilot cannot establish lasting relationships or health effects.

## Evidence status

- [Johnson et al., Community of Voices trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30412233/): 390 older adults at 12 cluster-randomized centers; at six months, the choir group improved on loneliness and interest in life relative to a wait-list group. Cognitive, physical and health-cost outcomes did not differ significantly. This supports studying particular experiences in that population; it does not isolate AI or generalize to every creative activity.
- [Tarr et al. (2015)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26510676/): an experiment manipulating dance synchrony and exertion found effects on reported bonding and pain threshold. It motivates investigating shared activity; short-term measures do not establish durable friendship, medical benefit or AI effectiveness.

These primary studies inform the questions, not predicted effects or the proposed sample size. No local feedback, baseline, venue, pilot or achieved benefit is claimed.

## What is needed to test it

A willing venue, a facilitator, participants who help review the design, an access budget, and an evaluator independent of the tool vendor. Agree data retention, consent for any recordings and ownership of created work. These resources are required, not secured; use anonymous summaries in public reporting.

## Who might make it happen

A library, arts organization or community association could host; a public or philanthropic commissioner could fund access. These are possible roles, not partners. Vendor sponsorship must not require AI use or suppress unfavorable results.

## What could make life worse

Generated material may stereotype a culture, displace a participant's contribution or create pressure to disclose personal beliefs. Noise, visual stimulation or unfamiliar tools can exclude people. Hosts must permit quiet participation, nonverbal contributions and withdrawal without explanation.

## Stop or rethink if

Pause the affected activity if consent is ignored, harassment occurs or access needs are unmet. Redesign if technology consumes the host's attention, increases participant burden or offers no valued improvement over the conventional group. Keep ordinary activities available during outages.

## Making this relevant in different lives

Review the design with people with different mobility, sensory and language needs, caregivers and people unable to afford transport. Do not assume religious participation, dancing, public performance or being recorded is welcome. Publish who could not participate and why, without identifying them.

## First contribution wanted

Ask prospective participants and a community host whether these three outcomes describe something missing in their lives, and which practical barrier should be removed first. Then cost the conventional activity before adding tools.
